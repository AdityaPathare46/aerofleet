\begin{abstract}
Flight logs are increasingly expected to explain why a small drone crashed. We tested that expectation on the public
PX4 Flight Review archive (479,547 logs; 590 crashes labelled by their uploaders as pilot- or aircraft-caused),
committing each test and its pass mark to version control before running it. A fixed attribution rule reached a balanced accuracy of 0.47 on 112
held-out crashes, and a cross-validated model stayed between 0.42 and 0.52. Checks that seemed to find the cause
named by forum experts mostly fired on the crash itself (tuning faults: 13 of 15 found, 2 of 10 without the last
10\,s). The crash flight did not differ from the same drone's earlier flights, but drones that
later crashed vibrated more on ordinary flights than matched drones (mean rank 0.67, 95\% CI 0.57--0.77,
Holm-adjusted $p=0.016$), though a replication on the newer accelerometer metric alone did not confirm it
(0.58, $p=0.25$, 19 sets). Small language-model panels lost to a fixed rule and were easily steered, and matching a
written problem description against 90 expert-diagnosed crashes found the expert's cause less often than the base
rate (20\% vs 32\%). An open-source fleet-assurance tool built on these findings presents evidence, not verdicts:
an event timeline, a fleet vibration ranking, a signed record, and 3D replay and rehearsal of flights. Flight logs support ranking airframes for inspection, not attributing an individual crash.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Small unmanned aircraft are now flown commercially for survey, inspection, agriculture and delivery, and the rules
that govern them place weight on records. In the European Union and the United Kingdom an operator in the specific
category must keep records of operations, maintenance and personnel for at least three years
\cite{eu2019947,easa2024ear}; India requires an accident to be reported within 48 hours \cite{india2021rules}, and
the United States within ten days \cite{faa107}. When something goes wrong, the onboard flight log is the richest
record available. Open-source log viewers, commercial fleet platforms and, more recently, machine-learning and
language-model prototypes promise to turn that log into an answer: what failed, and whether the pilot or the aircraft
was responsible \cite{duvvuru2025llm,silalahi2026droptc}.

The question matters beyond engineering curiosity. An investigation report, an insurance claim or a warranty
dispute often turns on whether a crash was caused by the person flying or by the aircraft, and an automatic verdict
that looks objective can carry weight it has not earned. Fleet operators face a related but different question: not
why one flight ended badly, but which of their drones are most likely to fail next and should be inspected first. A
log-based method that is good at the second task need not be good at the first, and confusing the two can mislead
both investigators and maintainers.

Whether logs can deliver an answer to the first question has, to our knowledge, not been tested on real crashes at scale. Public fault
datasets are dominated by simulation or by faults injected on purpose
\cite{keipour2021alfa,le2025rflymad,kabaoglu2026uavsead}, and accident statistics classify outcomes such as loss of
control rather than causes visible in a log \cite{wild2016drone,aaib2024review}. Two pitfalls make optimistic results
easy to obtain. First, a log that contains a crash also contains its consequences: saturated motors, extreme attitudes
and estimator errors. A detector can learn these instead of the cause, a form of data leakage
\cite{kaufman2012leakage,kapoor2023leakage}. Second, comparing crash flights with other operators' good flights mixes
the flight with the aircraft and the people using it, so a difference between groups need not say anything about the
flight that crashed.

This paper asks four questions of the largest public archive of real autopilot logs, PX4 Flight Review
\cite{meier2015px4,px4flightreview}:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[Q1] Do configuration settings differ between crash flights and normal flights?
\item[Q2] Can a log separate crashes caused by the pilot from crashes caused by the aircraft?
\item[Q3] Does the flight that crashed show warning signs relative to the same drone's history, or are the
differences between drones?
\item[Q4] Do expert diagnoses agree with uploader labels, and do automatic checks find the faults experts name?
\end{enumerate}
Because language-model assistants are now proposed for exactly this task, we also report a controlled test of small
local models used as a review panel.

The contributions are as follows. (i) We assemble a real-flight crash benchmark from public logs and check its labels
against expert diagnoses. (ii) Every confirmatory test was declared, with its threshold and pass mark, before the data
it used were analysed, and decisive tests were repeated on held-out data, following the logic of pre-registration
\cite{nosek2018prereg}. (iii) We show that crash attribution from logs was not achievable with these labels and
features, and that apparent diagnostic accuracy can come from the crash itself. (iv) We report one positive and
practically useful result: drones that later crash are rougher on their ordinary flights than matched drones, which
supports fleet-level inspection ranking but not per-flight prediction. (v) We show how these results translate
into the design of an open-source assurance tool, and report engineering checks of its parts, including one
negative result (Section~\ref{sec:tool}). Code, declarations, derived features and labels are released.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Fault datasets} Table~\ref{tab:datasets} compares our data with the main public resources. ALFA provides
47 fixed-wing flights with injected engine and control-surface failures \cite{keipour2021alfa}. RflyMAD covers 5,629
multicopter fault cases with eleven fault types, of which 497 are real flights and the rest software- or
hardware-in-the-loop simulations \cite{le2025rflymad}. UAV-SEAD offers about 1,396 real PX4 logs annotated for
state-estimation anomalies \cite{kabaoglu2026uavsead}, used by later anomaly-mining work \cite{wei2026aerotsboost};
a recent testbed quantifies the discrepancies between simulated and real PX4 logs \cite{kose2026testbed}. These
resources target anomaly or fault detection under known faults. None labels real crashes by responsibility, and none
separates the pre-crash window from the crash.

\paragraph{Fault detection and diagnosis} Surveys of fault detection for UAVs and robots cover model-based and
data-driven methods and stress how hard robotic diagnosis is in the field \cite{puchalski2022uav,khalastchi2018fdd}.
Causal models built from autopilot documentation have been used to find actual causes of failures in ArduPilot logs
\cite{zibaei2024causal}. A study of 569 bugs in PX4 and ArduPilot found parameter handling among the root-cause types
of drone-specific bugs \cite{wang2021autopilot}. Maintenance-oriented reliability analysis of UAVs models the passage
from partial degradation to failure \cite{petritoli2018reliability}, the view our between-drone result supports.

\paragraph{Accident analysis} Analyses of civil drone occurrences find equipment problems and loss of control
prominent \cite{wild2016drone}. Human-factors classifications of remotely piloted aircraft mishaps attribute a large
share of causal factors to people \cite{wiegmann2003hfacs,tvaryanas2006hfacs}. Such classifications depend on the
rater, which is one reason we check uploader labels against expert diagnoses.

\paragraph{Language models for diagnosis} Language-model agents have been applied to simulation testing and log
analytics for small UAVs \cite{duvvuru2025llm}, and learned models to classifying problems in drone log messages for
forensics \cite{silalahi2026droptc}. Multi-agent debate has been proposed to improve the factuality of model outputs
\cite{du2024debate}, while indirect prompt injection shows that text inside data can steer model behaviour
\cite{greshake2023injection}. We test both properties on our task.

\paragraph{Methodology} Leakage, where information unavailable at prediction time enters training or evaluation, is a
known source of irreproducible machine-learning results \cite{kaufman2012leakage,kapoor2023leakage}; label noise
likewise biases evaluation \cite{frenay2014label}. Matching reduces confounding in observational comparisons
\cite{stuart2010matching}, and pre-registration separates confirmatory from exploratory analysis
\cite{nosek2018prereg}. We apply these ideas to drone logs.

\paragraph{Tools for operators} Retrieving similar past cases and reusing their solutions is the basis of
case-based reasoning \cite{aamodt1994cbr}; ranking text by word overlap with BM25 is a standard, deterministic
retrieval method \cite{robertson2009bm25}. A hand-held miniature of the environment lets a person grasp and
inspect a spatial scene at a glance \cite{stoakley1995wim}, the idea behind the tabletop view in
Section~\ref{sec:tool}. We use these as engineering choices and test them only as far as stated.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Archive and labels}
PX4 Flight Review is a public service to which PX4 users upload onboard logs (ULog format) for plotting and sharing
\cite{px4flightreview}. We downloaded its public index on 20 October 2026: 479,547 logs (32,793,172 bytes,
SHA-256 \texttt{1bbb01c3\ldots a92d6}), of which 442,929 came from real hardware rather than simulation, from 38,320
distinct vehicle identifiers. Uploaders may rate a log. A total of 590 logs carry a crash rating, 402 attributing the
crash to software or hardware (``aircraft-caused'') and 188 to the pilot (``pilot-caused''). Logs rated ``good'' or
``great'' served as normal flights. Logs were parsed with pyulog. We store derived features and log identifiers, not
the logs.

A ULog file contains the parameters in force when logging started, any parameter changes during logging, timestamped
messages published by the autopilot's modules, and logged text messages with a severity level. The analyses below use
the vehicle status (arming state, navigation mode, failsafe flags), attitude and body-rate topics, rate setpoints,
inertial-sensor status, actuator outputs, battery status, GPS position and the land detector. The vehicle identifier
in the index is derived from the flight controller's hardware serial number; it identifies a flight controller, which
in practice usually means one airframe, though a controller can be moved between frames.

Crash-rated logs differ from well-rated logs in composition (Table~\ref{tab:composition}): they are shorter (median
54\,s against 388\,s, partly because a crash ends the log) and spread over more firmware generations. All between-group
comparisons below therefore use either matching or within-drone designs, and we do not pool across firmware
generations where measurements differ.

\subsection{Samples}
Figure~\ref{fig:flow} and Table~\ref{tab:samples} show how each analysis sample was drawn. Unless stated, crash
samples used real hardware, PX4 1.10 or newer and at least 5\,s of flight; normal flights were matched to crashes on
vehicle type and drawn from vehicles without any crash rating. For the within-drone test every crash with a vehicle
identifier and at least three earlier non-crash flights of 30\,s or more was eligible (101 crashes; up to the five most
recent earlier flights each). For the between-drone test each crashed drone was matched with up to two drones without
any crash-rated log that shared the flight-controller hardware, the PX4 major.minor version and the vehicle type
(66 matched sets, 124 control drones, three randomly chosen flights each, seed 7). Control drones were sampled
without replacement, so no control served two cases. The three exposure flights of a crashed drone were its most recent
non-crash flights dated on or before the crash; flights on the crash day were included in the declared test because the
archive does not give their order within a day, and removed in a sensitivity analysis.

\subsection{Expert diagnoses}
We searched the PX4 discussion forum for threads that link a Flight Review log and mention a crash (443 threads, 410
with a log link). For the 161 threads with a reply from PX4 staff, the development team or a member at the
forum's highest trust levels (148 of them describing a real crash), we recorded the expert's conclusion (aircraft, pilot, both, unclear, no diagnosis), the
cause named, who gave it, whether the original poster confirmed it, and the sentence stating the diagnosis. Recording
was AI-assisted and reviewed by an author; 123 of 124 recorded sentences were found verbatim in their thread by script.
The searches combined the Flight Review host names with the words crash, crashed, flyaway, fell, flipped and lost
control, and every returned thread was retrieved through the forum's public interface. A reply counted as expert when
its author was marked as staff, belonged to the development team group or held one of the forum's two highest trust
levels. Only public posts were used; usernames and post text are not redistributed.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Declaration protocol}
For each test, the selection, features, statistic, threshold and pass mark were committed to a public version-control
repository before the data it used were downloaded or analysed. Commit identifiers are listed in the repository's
paper notes. One amendment was made before any analysis: the firmware filter of the within-drone test was removed
because it left only 12 cases, and a fallback for older firmware was added. One coding error was corrected after a
first run (logs whose CPU-load record lacked the load field were being skipped); no threshold changed. The robustness
checks in Section~\ref{sec:robust} were added afterwards and are labelled exploratory.

\subsection{Crash moment and analysis window}
For rotary-wing aircraft the crash moment $t_c$ is the first time after take-off at which the tilt
$\theta=\arccos\left(1-2(q_x^2+q_y^2)\right)$, computed from the attitude quaternion, exceeds $60^\circ$; for fixed-wing
aircraft, and when no such tilt occurs, it is the end of the armed period. Normal flights use the end of the armed
period. Take-off is the first sample after arming at which the land detector reports the vehicle airborne. An analysis
with cut-off $m$ uses the window from take-off to $t_c-m$ and is skipped if the window is shorter than 10\,s.

\subsection{Features}
Table~\ref{tab:features} defines the features. Vibration is not measured the same way across firmware generations:
newer logs report an accelerometer vibration metric, older ones an estimator vibration estimate. Comparisons are
therefore only made between flights measured with the same source, and the source is recorded with every value. The
rate-tracking error falls back to the attitude message's body rates when the angular-velocity topic is absent.

\subsection{Tests}
\paragraph{Q1 settings} For logs that carry the firmware's record of default values, a protection was counted as
disabled when its value differed from the default and switched it off (for example a failsafe action of ``none'').
Crash and normal logs were compared with Fisher's exact test.

\paragraph{Q2 attribution} The declared rule labelled a crash ``aircraft'' if any of eight signs fired before $t_c$
(a critical battery warning or remaining charge below 10\%; any motor at 95\% or more for at least 1\,s in the last 10\,s;
more than 50 clipping events; poor GPS in a GPS-dependent mode; a failure-detector flag; an error-level message; CPU
load above 95\%; an active failsafe), otherwise ``pilot'' if the mode at $t_c$ was pilot-flown, otherwise
``unknown''. The pass mark was a balanced accuracy of at least 0.70 on decided cases, answering at least 60\% of
crashes and being wrong on at most 20\%. A model-based check used L2-regularised logistic regression on all features
($\lambda=1$, standardised inputs, median imputation within training folds), evaluated by stratified five-fold
cross-validation repeated five times and summarised by the ROC AUC \cite{hanley1982roc}.

The signs were chosen before the analysis to cover common failure modes of PX4 multirotors: power, propulsion,
vibration, navigation, software and failsafes. The
pilot rule was deliberately simple: if no aircraft sign fired and the vehicle was in a mode in which the pilot controls
attitude or position directly, the crash was attributed to the pilot. The rule was fixed before it was run on any crash log; only the list of message types available in the logs had
been inspected.

\paragraph{Q3a crash window} The same model separated crash from normal flights at cut-offs $m\in\{0,2,5,10,20,30\}$\,s.
We declared that the AUC would fall by at least 0.10 between $m=0$ and $m=10$\,s if crash consequences drove it.

\paragraph{Q3b and Q3c rank tests} For a crashed drone $i$ with value $x_{i0}$ and comparison values
$x_{i1},\dots,x_{in_i}$, the scaled rank is
\begin{equation}
r_i=\frac{1}{n_i}\sum_{j=1}^{n_i}\mathbf{1}\left[x_{ij}<x_{i0}\right],
\end{equation}
which is 0 when the crashed drone has the lowest value and 1 when it has the highest; ties count against the
hypothesis. A rank statistic was chosen for three reasons: the two vibration sources have different units and scales,
so only orderings are comparable across drones; the number of comparisons differs between drones, which the exact null
handles directly; and the distributions are skewed, with a few very rough flights, which would dominate a mean
difference. In the within-drone test (Q3b) $x_{i0}$ is the crash flight, cut 10\,s before $t_c$, and the comparisons
are the same drone's earlier flights, each cut 10\,s before the end of its armed period. In the between-drone test (Q3c) $x_{i0}$ is the median over the crashed drone's
three most recent pre-crash flights and the comparisons are the medians of the matched controls. Under no difference
each $r_i$ is uniform on $\{0,1/n_i,\dots,1\}$ with mean 0.5, and $\bar r$ estimates the probability that the crashed
drone exceeds a comparison, the quantity estimated by the Mann--Whitney statistic \cite{mann1947test}. One-sided
$p$-values come from 100,000 draws of this exact null; 95\% confidence intervals from 10,000 bootstrap resamples of
drones \cite{efron1993bootstrap}. Each test had two primary features (vibration, rate-tracking error) at
$\alpha=0.025$.

\paragraph{Q4 expert diagnoses} Expert conclusions were compared with uploader labels. Fixed checks for the fault the
expert named (tuning, vibration, GPS or compass, motor, battery) were declared useful if they fired on at least 50\% of
the logs with that diagnosis and at most 10\% of normal flights, first with the full window and then with $m=10$\,s.

\paragraph{Language-model panel} The panel tested a design that has been proposed for advisory diagnosis: several
reviewers, each restricted to one slice of evidence (navigation, pilot inputs, GPS, battery, settings), vote on a fixed
list of six causes and must cite numbered facts that code extracted from the log. Citations to facts outside a
reviewer's slice or to non-existent facts were discarded by code. In the second round each reviewer saw only the vote
counts of the first round, not the other reviewers' text, to limit persuasion. A cause was reported only if it had at
least one supporting vote, a net score of at least one and a lead of at least one over the runner-up; otherwise the
case was referred to a person. Sixty generated cases with a known cause (clear, subtle and no-cause cases, some with
distractors) were presented to five reviewers; a single agent shown all facts and the fixed rule served as
comparators. Four local models of 3 to 4 billion parameters ran at temperature 0. Robustness was
measured by replacing one reviewer with an adversary arguing for a chosen wrong cause. Separately, eight hostile
strings were planted in five uploader-controlled fields to test whether text could reach a prompt.

\paragraph{Multiplicity} Seven confirmatory tests produce $p$-values. Holm's procedure \cite{holm1979} is reported over
all seven in addition to each test's declared $\alpha$.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Configuration settings (Q1)}
Of 57 crash logs and 113 normal logs carrying a firmware-default record, 29 (51\%) and 52 (46\%) had at least one
protection disabled relative to the default (Fisher $p=0.63$). Pilot-caused crashes had a protection disabled more
often than aircraft-caused ones (14 of 20 against 15 of 37), a contrast too small to test. None of 100 crash logs
changed a safety-critical or flight-limit setting during the flight.

\subsection{Attributing a crash to the pilot or the aircraft (Q2)}
The fixed rule reached a balanced accuracy of 0.57 on 93 development crashes (35\% wrong) and 0.47 on 112 held-out
crashes (47\% wrong), against the declared 0.70, and labelled 39\% of 143 normal flights as aircraft faults
(Table~\ref{tab:tests}). The signs fired at similar rates whichever party the uploader blamed
(Fig.~\ref{fig:signs}): motor saturation in 23\% of aircraft-caused and 29\% of pilot-caused crashes (1\% of normal
flights), battery warnings in 15\% and 18\% (3\%). Error messages and failsafes were no more frequent in crashes than
in normal flights. Two signals noticed after the development run did not replicate on held-out crashes: accelerometer
clipping (24\% against 8\%, $p=0.045$) and manual mode at the crash (18\% against 10\%, $p=0.165$). The
cross-validated model stayed between 0.42 and 0.52 at every cut-off (Fig.~\ref{fig:leak}).

\subsection{The crash window (Q3a)}
\label{sec:window}
Crash flights were separated from normal flights with an AUC of 0.88 (SD 0.01) using the whole flight and 0.86 with the
last 30\,s removed (Fig.~\ref{fig:leak}); the declared drop of 0.10 by 10\,s was not observed (0.01). Leakage was
nevertheless present for individual checks. The rate-tracking feature alone fell from an AUC of 0.81 to 0.69 as the
crash was excluded, while vibration stayed between 0.74 and 0.77. A fixed tuning check that found 13 of 15
expert-diagnosed tuning faults with the crash included found 2 of 10 once the last 10\,s were removed
(Section~\ref{sec:experts}). Model-level separation therefore does not depend on the crash window; it reflects
differences that are present throughout the flight. Table~\ref{tab:features_auc} gives the single-feature AUCs. Values
below 0.5 indicate features that are lower in crash flights: crash flights reached lower tilt and smaller stick
deflections over the whole flight than normal flights, consistent with shorter and less aggressive flights that end abruptly.

\subsection{Within the same drone and between drones (Q3b, Q3c)}
Relative to the same drone's earlier flights, the crash flight was neither more vibrating ($\bar r=0.48$, 95\% CI
0.38--0.58, $p=0.67$, 61 drones) nor worse at tracking its commands ($\bar r=0.50$, 0.38--0.63, $p=0.47$, 46 drones;
Fig.~\ref{fig:within}). Relative to matched drones without a recorded crash, drones that later crashed vibrated more on
their ordinary pre-crash flights ($\bar r=0.67$, 0.57--0.77, $p=0.0023$, 60 sets; Holm-adjusted $p=0.016$), while the
tracking error did not differ significantly ($\bar r=0.57$, 0.44--0.70, $p=0.15$, 47 sets). The vibration difference
was clearest for aircraft-caused crashes ($\bar r=0.67$, $p=0.005$, 46 sets) and similar in size but not significant
for pilot-caused ones ($\bar r=0.64$, $p=0.16$, 14 sets). The crash-versus-normal separation of the previous
subsection is therefore a property of the drone rather than a build-up within the flight that crashed.

\subsection{Robustness of the between-drone result (exploratory)}
\label{sec:robust}
The vibration result held when controls were restricted to the same airframe as the case ($\bar r=0.69$, $p=0.008$,
39 sets), when case flights from the crash day were removed ($\bar r=0.76$, $p=0.003$, 25 sets) and after adjusting
for flight duration ($\bar r=0.69$, $p=0.0005$, 57 sets; Fig.~\ref{fig:robust}). Duration was barely correlated with
vibration (Spearman $\rho=0.05$ and $-0.04$ for the two measurement sources), although case flights were longer than
control flights (median 289\,s against 173\,s). The effect was carried by the older estimator vibration estimate
($\bar r=0.68$, $p=0.005$, 48 sets); with the newer accelerometer metric alone the direction was the same but the sample
was too small to decide ($\bar r=0.62$, $p=0.19$, 12 sets). A declared replication on the newer metric only,
admitting every newer-firmware crashed drone with at least one earlier flight (26 sets selected, 19 usable), did not
confirm the effect: $\bar r=0.58$ (95\% CI 0.39--0.76, $p=0.25$); on the ten cases not in the original test it was
0.50. The interval is compatible both with no effect and with the effect seen on the older estimate, so whether the
newer metric shows the same difference remains open. The within-drone null did not depend on the crash-moment
threshold ($\bar r=0.46$ at $45^\circ$, $0.50$ at $75^\circ$). As a screening signal the effect is moderate: the
crashed drone was the roughest of its matched set in 55\% of sets, against 41\% expected without a difference.

\section{Design implications: an assurance tool built on the findings}
\label{sec:tool}

The authors develop AeroFleet, an open-source, ground-side assurance tool for PX4 and ArduPilot fleets. It reads
the files an operator already has (parameter files, mission files and flight logs), contacts no aircraft and calls no
language model. Table~\ref{tab:design} links each result above to a design decision and to the check that was made of
it. The injection and leave-one-out checks were declared before they were run, like the tests above; the others are
software tests. All are engineering validation; none evaluates the tool as a safety intervention.

\centering

\paragraph{Evidence, not verdicts} A flight review reads an ArduPilot or PX4 log and the approved mission file and
lists, in time order, mode changes (with a pilot taking over from an automatic mode flagged), settings changed in
flight, battery and voltage warnings, poor GPS and deviations from the plan. It reports the quality of that evidence
(sample rate, satellite count and dilution of precision, height reference, clock source) so that a reader can judge
how far to trust it, and leaves the conclusion to a person. Given the attribution results, a tool that issued a
pilot-or-aircraft verdict would claim more than the log supports.

\paragraph{Ranking drones, not predicting flights} Each reviewed flight's vibration is stored with its measurement
source; drones are ranked against others measured the same way, and those above their group's upper quartile are
flagged for mechanical inspection. In the archive, this kind of ranking identified the drone that later crashed as
the roughest of its matched set in 55\% of sets, against 41\% by chance, so the flag is a prompt to inspect, not a
warning that a crash is coming.

\paragraph{A record that can be checked} Because the useful evidence accumulates per aircraft, results are appended to
a hash-chained record in which each entry is signed and carries the SHA-256 of the exact files it came from; each new
head is also written to a separate file intended to be copied elsewhere. A standalone verifier checks an exported
record; in tests it detected an edited field, a deleted entry, a reordering, and a chain rebuilt with a new key
(the last only against a pinned key fingerprint or the separately kept heads, which is the stated limit of the design).

\subsection{Planned versus flown}
\label{sec:tool_reconcile}
The check aligns the flown track with the plan's legs in flying order over the whole flight and reports stretches
beyond the fleet's sideways and height tolerances (defaults 30\,m and 15\,m for at least 3\,s). To test it, plans were
built from 40 real multirotor flights in the archive (a waypoint every 10\,s of track), so that the unmodified
flight conformed by construction, and a known deviation was injected for 30\,s. Sideways shifts of 35\,m or more were
found in 90--98\% of flights and upward shifts of 20\,m or more in 92--98\%, while shifts below the tolerances were
mostly not reported, as specified. The first version of the check, which matched each position to the nearest leg
anywhere, raised false alarms on 16 of 40 unmodified flights and detected only half of the 35\,m shifts;
it was replaced. The current version raised false alarms on 9 of 40 (22\%, 95\% CI 12--38\%), all on fast flights
cutting the corners of long legs built from the track; with a waypoint every 5\,s (a post-hoc check) there were none.
A declared check on the archive logs that record the mission they flew found
only six distinct missions; the check reported no deviation while any of them flew in mission mode, and the two
deviations it did report were stretches in which the vehicle had left mission mode (return-to-launch, or the pilot
taking over). Six missions are too few for a rate (95\% upper bound 39\%), so the false-alarm rate on real plans
remains unmeasured and a reported deviation is a reason to look at the replay rather than a finding.

\subsection{Searching past diagnoses}
\label{sec:tool_search}
An operator can describe a problem in words. The description is matched (BM25, no model) against the expert-labelled
forum threads, represented by their titles and up to 30 descriptive terms from the opening post, and against a short
list of symptom words for each cause. After a first release we declared and ran a test, holding out each of the 90 threads in
which the expert blamed the aircraft and named a cause and querying with the rest. The cause the words matched best
was the expert's cause in 17 of 90 cases with the title alone (19\%) and 18 of 90 with the title and opening-post
terms (20\%, 95\% CI 13--29\%), below the 29 of 90 (32\%) of always answering the most frequent cause; among the first
three it was 53\% against 66\%. The declared criterion (10 points above the base rate) was not met. A written
description, like the log, did not reveal the cause. The tool therefore orders its checklist by how often experts
found each cause, marks the causes the description mentions, and offers the most similar threads only as reading,
with the measured accuracy shown beside the answer.

\subsection{Three-dimensional replay and rehearsal}
\label{sec:tool_vr}
Deviations, heights and separation between drones are spatial, and a two-dimensional plot hides height. The tool
therefore draws flights on a virtual tabletop, a world in miniature \cite{stoakley1995wim}, that can be viewed in a
browser, in immersive virtual reality or, on a passthrough headset, in mixed reality on a real desk (WebXR).
A \emph{flight replay} shows a reviewed flight: the approved plan dashed, the flown track green within tolerance and
red during a deviation, the log's events pinned where they happened, and a playhead that can be scrubbed or played at
ten times real speed. A \emph{mission rehearsal} shows planned missions before anyone flies: the deconfliction check
turns each mission file into a position-over-time trajectory on a common clock (straight legs at stated climb, cruise
and descent speeds), finds every pair closer than the separation distance and resolves conflicts by a launch delay or,
where allowed, a different cruise height. The rehearsal flies all missions on that clock, as uploaded and as
rescheduled, with each loss of separation drawn where and when it occurs (Fig.~\ref{fig:rehearsal}). It is a kinematic rehearsal of the plan, not
a simulation of the aircraft: there is no wind, no vehicle dynamics and no sensor error, so it shows what the plan
implies if it is flown as written, which is the assumption the planned-versus-flown check then tests after the
flight. The replay and rehearsal have been checked functionally on desktop; a paired standalone headset application
compiles but has not been run on a headset, and no user study has been carried out, so we make no claim that the
three-dimensional view improves decisions.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Attribution} A declared rule, its held-out replication and a cross-validated model all found no usable
separation between pilot- and aircraft-caused crashes. Uploader labels are noisy, but the expert threads suggest they
are not random: where an expert reached a verdict on an aircraft-rated crash, it agreed. A more likely explanation is
that a pilot error and an aircraft fault leave similar traces in the signals a log records, which is also suggested by
how often experts themselves remained unclear. Tools that issue automatic responsibility verdicts from logs should be
evaluated against independent labels before use in investigations or claims.

\paragraph{Leakage} The model-level leakage we expected was absent, yet single checks showed it clearly: a tuning check
looked accurate only because the crash was in its window. Evaluations of log-based diagnosis should report results with
the crash window removed, and should compare against the same aircraft's history before claiming that a signal
anticipates a crash.

\paragraph{Drone-level risk} The positive result concerns airframes, not flights. Drones that later crashed vibrated
more than comparable drones on ordinary flights, and expert diagnoses most often named tuning, configuration and
propulsion problems, which raise vibration. This is consistent with a maintenance view of UAV reliability in which
degraded condition precedes failure \cite{petritoli2018reliability}. It supports ranking a fleet's drones by vibration
measured the same way and inspecting the roughest; it does not support a per-flight crash warning, the screening
gain is moderate, and the effect rests on the older vibration estimate: a replication on the newer metric was
inconclusive.

\paragraph{From findings to a tool} Section~\ref{sec:tool} turns these results into design decisions for an
operator's tool. The common thread is that the tool shows evidence and ranks drones, and does not name a cause.

\paragraph{Language models} On this task small local models added errors and an attack surface rather than accuracy.
A debate-style panel, related to multi-agent debate \cite{du2024debate}, did not help at this model size, and the adversarial result echoes concerns about
steering through injected content \cite{greshake2023injection}. Larger models were not tested.

\paragraph{Relation to prior datasets} Benchmarks built from injected faults \cite{keipour2021alfa,le2025rflymad}
answer whether a known fault can be detected; anomaly datasets from real logs \cite{kabaoglu2026uavsead} answer
whether unusual segments can be found. Our results concern a different question, attribution of a real crash, and
suggest that performance on the first two does not transfer to it: signals that mark a fault also mark the crash it
causes and the pilot's reaction to it. Datasets that pair real crashes with independently verified causes would be
needed to make progress on attribution, and our expert-diagnosis labels are a small first step.

\paragraph{Records and regulation} Record-keeping rules ask operators to keep logs, maintenance records and
occurrence reports \cite{eu2019947,easa2024ear}. On our evidence, such records are most useful when they are
kept per aircraft over time, so that an airframe's condition can be compared with its own history and with comparable
airframes, rather than examined only after an accident.

\paragraph{Practical guidance} For operators and investigators we suggest: (i) treat automatic pilot-or-aircraft
verdicts from logs with caution; (ii) use logs to assemble a time-ordered record of events for a human investigator;
(iii) track vibration per drone across flights and inspect drones that are rough relative to comparable airframes;
(iv) when evaluating any log-based diagnostic, remove the crash window and compare against the same drone's history.

\paragraph{Future work} Three studies have been declared in the repository before any data were collected: a
blind second coding of the 161 forum threads by a co-author who has not seen the first recording, with agreement
reported as Cohen's kappa (pass mark 0.60); a prospective test in partner fleets, in which the monthly vibration
ranking is written to the signed record before outcomes occur and crashes, unplanned repairs and defects found at
inspection are compared between flagged and unflagged drones over six months; and a within-subjects user study
comparing the three-dimensional views with a two-dimensional map on tasks with known answers. Repeating the
between-drone analysis on ArduPilot fleets, and a larger set of crashes with causes verified by independent
investigators, would further strengthen the evidence.

\section{Threats to validity}
\label{sec:threats}

\paragraph{Labels} Ratings are the uploaders' own and normal flights are self-rated. Controls without a crash-rated log
may have crashed without a rating; this misclassification biases the between-drone test towards no difference, so the
observed effect is, if anything, conservative. Forum diagnoses were recorded with AI assistance by one recorder,
verified by quotation, and half of the expert-diagnosed logs are no longer public.

\paragraph{Selection} The archive is self-selected, PX4-only and rich in development and test flights, and crash-rated
logs differ from well-rated ones in duration and firmware era (Table~\ref{tab:composition}). Results may not transfer to
commercial ArduPilot fleets or to proprietary autopilots.

\paragraph{Measurement} The crash moment is estimated; the within-drone result did not depend on the threshold. The
between-drone effect was shown mainly with the older vibration estimate and was not confirmed on the newer metric
in a declared replication with 19 usable sets, and matching used hardware, firmware, vehicle
type and, in a sensitivity analysis, airframe, but not propellers, payloads or missions.

\paragraph{Power} Some comparisons are small (14 pilot-caused matched sets, 12 sets with the newer vibration metric,
three vibration-diagnosed logs). Null results there are not evidence of no effect.

\paragraph{Language models} The panel cases are generated, not real incidents, and were written by the same authors as
the fixed rule, so the rule's score is closer to a training score than a test score; a blind set of cases written by
someone who has not seen the rule is in preparation. Only small models were tested.

\paragraph{The tool} The tool's checks are engineering validation on archive data, not field evaluation. Plans in the
injection test were built from the flights themselves; the description search was tested on forum titles, which are
shorter and more varied than an operator's report, and its symptom lists were written by the authors; the vibration
ranking has not been tested prospectively; and the three-dimensional views have had no user study.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
On real, public crash logs, configuration settings did not distinguish crashes, neither rules nor a model could tell
whether the pilot or the aircraft was responsible, and the flight that crashed looked like the same drone's earlier
flights. What the logs did reveal, on the older vibration estimate, was which drones were rougher than their peers;
whether the newer metric shows the same remains open. Log analysis for operators is better
aimed at fleet-level inspection ranking and at presenting evidence to a human investigator, and claims of automatic
crash attribution should be tested with the crash window removed and against each aircraft's own history. A tool
built on these results can be useful without naming causes: it can order the evidence, rank the drones that deserve
inspection, keep a record that can be checked, and let a person see a flight or a plan in three dimensions.
