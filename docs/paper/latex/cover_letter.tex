\begin{letter}{The Editor-in-Chief\\ Reliability Engineering \& System Safety}
\opening{Dear Editor,}

We submit the manuscript ``Flight logs identify crash-prone drones but not crash causes: pre-declared tests on
479,547 public PX4 logs'' for consideration as a research article.

Flight logs are increasingly used to decide why a small drone crashed and who was responsible. We tested that use on
the largest public archive of real autopilot logs. Every confirmatory test was committed to version control, with its
threshold, before the data were analysed, and decisive tests were repeated on held-out data. The results are of direct
relevance to the journal's readership in safety and reliability:
\begin{itemize}
\item neither a declared rule nor a cross-validated model could separate pilot-caused from aircraft-caused crashes;
\item checks that appear to diagnose a fault can owe their accuracy to the crash itself (a form of data leakage);
\item the flight that crashed was no different from the same drone's earlier flights, whereas drones that later
crashed vibrated more than matched drones on ordinary flights, a result that survived correction for multiple testing
and several robustness checks;
\item small language-model review panels performed worse than a fixed rule and could be steered by one compromised
reviewer.
\end{itemize}
Together these findings separate what logs can support (ranking airframes for inspection) from what they cannot
(attributing an individual crash), and they give practical guidance for evaluating log-based diagnostics. Code,
declarations, derived features and labels are released.

The manuscript is original, has not been published and is not under consideration elsewhere. All authors approve the
submission. An AI assistant was used in preparing code and text, as declared in the manuscript.
